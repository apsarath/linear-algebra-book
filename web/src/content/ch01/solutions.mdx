<SolutionEntry to="rev-parallelogram-area">

With one vertex at the origin, the four vertices are $\vv{0} = \colvec{0\\0}$, $\vv{u} = \colvec{3\\0}$, $\vv{v} = \colvec{1\\4}$, and $\vv{u}+\vv{v} = \colvec{4\\4}$.

<Figure src="ch01/sol-parallelogram-area.svg" alt="Parallelogram with vertices (0,0), (3,0), (4,4), and (1,4). The base along the x-axis has length 3; a dashed vertical marks the height 4." />

Take the side along $\vv{u}$ as the **base**. That side runs from $(0,0)$ to $(3,0)$ on the $x$-axis, so its length is $\norm{\vv{u}} = 3$.

The opposite side runs from $(1,4)$ to $(4,4)$: a horizontal line at $y = 4$. The **height** is the perpendicular distance between these two parallel sides. Because both are horizontal, that distance is just the difference of the $y$-coordinates, which is $4$. (Equivalently: drop a perpendicular from the tip of $\vv{v}$ to the $x$-axis; it lands at $(1,0)$ and has length $4$.)

The area of a parallelogram is base times height:

$$
3 \times 4 = 12.
$$

The same number shows up if you *shear* the figure: slide the top side left by $1$ unit, and the parallelogram becomes a $3$ by $4$ rectangle. A shear preserves area, and the rectangle's area is $12$ too.

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="rev-parallelogram-law">

Using the result of <Ref to="rev-norm-properties" />:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\norm{\vv{a}+\vv{b}}^2 &= \norm{\vv{a}}^2 + 2\textstyle\sum a_ib_i + \norm{\vv{b}}^2,\\
\norm{\vv{a}-\vv{b}}^2 &= \norm{\vv{a}}^2 - 2\textstyle\sum a_ib_i + \norm{\vv{b}}^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding: $\norm{\vv{a}+\vv{b}}^2 + \norm{\vv{a}-\vv{b}}^2 = 2\norm{\vv{a}}^2 + 2\norm{\vv{b}}^2$.

*Geometric interpretation:* In a parallelogram with sides $\vv{a}$ and $\vv{b}$, $\vv{a}+\vv{b}$ and $\vv{a}-\vv{b}$ are the two diagonals. The identity says: the sum of the squares of the diagonals equals the sum of the squares of all four sides.

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="rev-nearest-point">

Distances are never negative, and for nonnegative numbers a smaller square means a smaller number. So the $t$ that makes $\norm{\vv{p} - t\vv{v}}^2$ smallest also makes $\norm{\vv{p} - t\vv{v}}$ smallest, and we can work with the square, which has no square root in it:

$$
\norm{\vv{p} - t\vv{v}}^2 = \norm{\colvec{5-t\\1-2t}}^2 = (5-t)^2 + (1-2t)^2 = 25 - 10t + t^2 + 1 - 4t + 4t^2 = 5t^2 - 14t + 26.
$$

To find where this quadratic is smallest, **complete the square**. Factor $5$ out of the terms that involve $t$:

$$
5t^2 - 14t + 26 = 5\left(t^2 - \tfrac{14}{5}t\right) + 26.
$$

Half of $\tfrac{14}{5}$ is $\tfrac{7}{5}$, and $\left(t - \tfrac{7}{5}\right)^2 = t^2 - \tfrac{14}{5}t + \tfrac{49}{25}$. So $t^2 - \tfrac{14}{5}t = \left(t - \tfrac{7}{5}\right)^2 - \tfrac{49}{25}$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
5t^2 - 14t + 26 &= 5\left[\left(t - \tfrac{7}{5}\right)^2 - \tfrac{49}{25}\right] + 26 \\
&= 5\left(t - \tfrac{7}{5}\right)^2 - \tfrac{49}{5} + 26 \\
&= 5\left(t - \tfrac{7}{5}\right)^2 + \tfrac{81}{5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now read off the answer. The term $5\left(t - \tfrac{7}{5}\right)^2$ is never negative, and it equals $0$ exactly when $t = \tfrac{7}{5}$. So the squared distance is always at least $\tfrac{81}{5}$, and it reaches $\tfrac{81}{5}$ only at

$$
t = \frac{7}{5}.
$$

The same rewriting works for any quadratic $f(t) = at^2 + bt + c$ with $a > 0$. Factor $a$ out of the terms that involve $t$, then add and subtract the square of half the remaining coefficient of $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(t) &= a\left(t^2 + \tfrac{b}{a}t\right) + c \\
&= a\left[\left(t + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \left(\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2\right] + c \\
&= a\left(t + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The leftover $c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}$ does not depend on $t$. The other term is a square times $a$, so (because $a > 0$) it is never negative, and it equals $0$ exactly when $t + \tfrac{b}{2a} = 0$. Therefore $f$ is smallest at

$$
t = -\frac{b}{2a},
$$

and that is the only such $t$. If $a$ were negative the same square would open the other way, and this $t$ would be a *maximum*.

In our problem $a = 5$, $b = -14$, and $c = 26$, so

$$
t = -\frac{-14}{2\cdot 5} = \frac{14}{10} = \frac{7}{5},
$$

which matches the completing-the-square computation above. The formula is a shortcut once you have seen why it works; the rewriting into $a\left(t + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \text{constant}$ is the reason.

The nearest point is $\frac{7}{5}\colvec{1\\2} = \colvec{7/5\\14/5}$, and the shortest distance is $\sqrt{81/5} = 9/\sqrt{5} \approx 4.02$.

<Figure src="ch01/sol-nearest-point.svg" alt="The line through the origin along v = (1,2), the point p = (5,1), and the nearest point (7/5, 14/5) on the line. The dashed segment from the nearest point to p meets the line at a right angle." />

Look at the picture: the segment from the nearest point to $\vv{p}$ meets the line at a **right angle**. You can check this with the slopes. The line has slope $2$, and the segment from $\left(\tfrac{7}{5}, \tfrac{14}{5}\right)$ to $(5, 1)$ has slope $\dfrac{1 - 14/5}{5 - 7/5} = \dfrac{-9/5}{18/5} = -\tfrac{1}{2}$. The product of the slopes is $-1$, so they are perpendicular. This is no accident. The closest point on a line is always found by dropping a perpendicular, an idea we will develop into *projections* in Chapter 11.

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="ch-diff-norm" title="Length of u − v">

By the parallelogram law (<Ref to="rev-parallelogram-law" />), $\norm{\vv{u}+\vv{v}}^2 + \norm{\vv{u}-\vv{v}}^2 = 2\norm{\vv{u}}^2 + 2\norm{\vv{v}}^2$.

Substituting: $25 + \norm{\vv{u}-\vv{v}}^2 = 2(9) + 2(16) = 50$.

Therefore $\norm{\vv{u}-\vv{v}}^2 = 25$, giving $\norm{\vv{u}-\vv{v}} = 5$.

(Bonus observation: $\norm{\vv{u}}=3$, $\norm{\vv{v}}=4$, $\norm{\vv{u}+\vv{v}}=5$ is a 3-4-5 right triangle, so $\vv{u} \perp \vv{v}$. The parallelogram is a rectangle, and both diagonals have the same length.)

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="ch-nontrivial-zero" title="Nontrivial combination giving the zero vector">

Subtract: $\vv{0} = \vv{b} - \vv{b} = (c_1-d_1)\vv{v}_1 + (c_2-d_2)\vv{v}_2 + \cdots + (c_k-d_k)\vv{v}_k$.

Since $(c_1,\ldots,c_k) \ne (d_1,\ldots,d_k)$, there exists at least one index $i$ with $c_i \ne d_i$, so $c_i - d_i \ne 0$. This gives a linear combination of $\vv{v}_1,\ldots,\vv{v}_k$ that equals $\vv{0}$ with at least one nonzero coefficient—a nontrivial linear combination.

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="ch-angle-bisector" title="Angle bisector">

Write $\hat{\vv{a}} = \vv{a}/\norm{\vv{a}}$ and $\hat{\vv{b}} = \vv{b}/\norm{\vv{b}}$. Then

$$
\vv{c} = \norm{\vv{b}}\,\vv{a} + \norm{\vv{a}}\,\vv{b} = \norm{\vv{a}}\norm{\vv{b}}(\hat{\vv{a}} + \hat{\vv{b}}).
$$

Since $\norm{\vv{a}}\norm{\vv{b}} > 0$, the direction of $\vv{c}$ is the same as the direction of $\hat{\vv{a}} + \hat{\vv{b}}$.

Now $\hat{\vv{a}}$ and $\hat{\vv{b}}$ are unit vectors. The vector $\hat{\vv{a}} + \hat{\vv{b}}$ is the diagonal of a *rhombus* (a parallelogram with all sides of length 1) with sides $\hat{\vv{a}}$ and $\hat{\vv{b}}$. The diagonal of a rhombus bisects its vertex angles. Therefore $\hat{\vv{a}} + \hat{\vv{b}}$—and hence $\vv{c}$—bisects the angle between $\vv{a}$ and $\vv{b}$.

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="ch-polarization" title="Polarization identity">

Expand both norms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\norm{\vv{u}+\vv{v}}^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n(u_i+v_i)^2 = \sum u_i^2 + 2\sum u_iv_i + \sum v_i^2, \\
\norm{\vv{u}-\vv{v}}^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n(u_i-v_i)^2 = \sum u_i^2 - 2\sum u_iv_i + \sum v_i^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting: $\norm{\vv{u}+\vv{v}}^2 - \norm{\vv{u}-\vv{v}}^2 = 4\sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i$.

</SolutionEntry>

<SolutionEntry to="ch-centroid" title="Centroid minimizes the sum of squared distances">

Let $f(\vv{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^m \norm{\vv{x}-\vv{v}_i}^2$. Write $\vv{x} = \vv{g} + \vv{d}$ where $\vv{d} = \vv{x} - \vv{g}$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(\vv{x}) &= \sum_{i=1}^m \norm{(\vv{g}+\vv{d})-\vv{v}_i}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^m \norm{(\vv{g}-\vv{v}_i)+\vv{d}}^2 \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^m \bigl[\norm{\vv{g}-\vv{v}_i}^2 + 2(\vv{g}-\vv{v}_i)\cdot\vv{d} + \norm{\vv{d}}^2\bigr] \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^m\norm{\vv{g}-\vv{v}_i}^2 + 2\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^m(\vv{g}-\vv{v}_i)\Bigr)\cdot\vv{d} + m\norm{\vv{d}}^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

where we write $\vv{p}\cdot\vv{q} = \sum_j p_jq_j$.

Now $\sum_{i=1}^m(\vv{g}-\vv{v}_i) = m\vv{g} - \sum\vv{v}_i = m\vv{g} - m\vv{g} = \vv{0}$ (by definition of $\vv{g}$).

So $f(\vv{x}) = f(\vv{g}) + m\norm{\vv{d}}^2 \ge f(\vv{g})$, with equality iff $\vv{d}=\vv{0}$, i.e., $\vv{x}=\vv{g}$. Thus the centroid uniquely minimizes the sum of squared distances.

</SolutionEntry>
